## 28 – Before the Wood Is Carved

The old artisans knew how to look at an uncarved block of wood without hurrying to decide what it should become. Within it slept a bowl, perhaps, or a spoon, or nothing that needed making at all. The Sage carries something of this patience. She knows strength, yet does not fear tenderness. She has walked beneath the brightness of noon, yet remains at home beneath the dark moon. She knows her own face, yet does not imagine that the world must resemble it. Holding these opposites without grasping either, she becomes spacious enough to receive life as it comes. Perhaps this is why she resembles a child. Before we divided the world into useful and useless, strong and weak, worthy and unworthy, there was simply wonder. The primal self still remembers that wide country, where nothing needs to become its opposite before it can belong.

I have sometimes wondered whether the Friend was leading us toward that country when he placed a child among those arguing about greatness. The child had no throne, no doctrine of importance, no carefully carved identity to defend. There was only this small life, receiving the moment with open hands. The Beloved seems forever drawing us back toward such simplicity. We spend years carving ourselves into shapes the world will recognize—successful, wise, righteous, admired—until we forget the living wood beneath the carving. Yet grace moves deeper than these names. It finds us before our accomplishments and beneath our failures, in that quiet place where we are simply held. Perhaps to become new is also to become ancient: to return beneath everything we have made of ourselves and discover the image that was there before the knife touched the wood.

> *Return me to the life beneath my names.*
