## Preface

I have long been drawn to the wisdom of the Dao.

There is something about the yin-yang symbol that I find deeply beautiful. Two movements curling into one another, darkness carrying a seed of light and light carrying a seed of darkness. Neither destroys the other. Neither possesses the whole. Together they suggest a reality held not by the elimination of opposites, but by a harmony spacious enough to contain them.

And then there is *wu wei*—one of those ideas that seems wonderfully simple until I try to live it.

Often translated as “non-action” or “effortless action,” *wu wei* does not mean doing nothing. I have come to hear it more as acting without forcing, learning the grain of life and moving with it rather than continually trying to bend reality toward my will. Water does this. It does not argue with the mountain, yet over centuries it carves the valley. A tree does this when it bends beneath the wind rather than breaking against it.

I find this beautiful.

I also find it very difficult.

I am a follower of Jesus. Christianity is the spiritual language in which I learned to pray, the landscape in which I continue to encounter God, and the story through which I most naturally understand love, grace, surrender, death, and resurrection. Yet I have never believed that wisdom belongs exclusively to one culture. If God is the source of all truth, then I should not be surprised to discover traces of wisdom wherever human beings have looked deeply into life with open hands and attentive hearts.

That conviction is what gave birth to this book.

The *Dao De Jing*, also written *Daodejing* and historically known simply as the *Laozi*, is one of the foundational texts of Chinese philosophy. Tradition attributes it to Laozi—“the Old Master”—who was said to have lived around the sixth century BCE. The historical picture is much less certain. Modern scholarship generally places much of the text's formation in the fourth and third centuries BCE, likely drawing upon sayings and traditions that circulated earlier and were gathered and refined over time. Ancient manuscript discoveries at Guodian and Mawangdui have shown that different forms and arrangements of the text circulated before the familiar eighty-one-chapter version became established.

Perhaps that uncertainty is appropriate for a book whose opening lines warn us that the Way that can be captured in words is not the enduring Way.

The historical Laozi remains elusive as well. Chinese tradition remembers a wise old teacher, sometimes associated with the Zhou court, whose wisdom eventually became identified with the *Dao De Jing*. Scholars debate whether Laozi was one historical individual, whether traditions associated with such a teacher were later gathered under his name, or whether “Laozi” eventually came to personify a broader stream of ancient wisdom.

For the purposes of this book, I am comfortable allowing some mystery to remain.

What matters to me is the voice that has come down through the centuries.

It is a voice suspicious of striving.

A voice that prefers water to stone, valleys to mountaintops, yielding to domination, simplicity to excess, silence to endless explanation.

It speaks of the *Dao*—the Way—as something that cannot finally be named or possessed, yet from which the ten thousand things arise. It speaks of *de*, often translated as virtue or power, not primarily as moral performance but as a kind of integrity that appears when a life becomes aligned with the Way. And again and again it returns to *wu wei*: action that does not force, leadership that does not dominate, accomplishment that does not cling to its accomplishments. These are among the central currents running through the text.

As I read these words, I sometimes find myself thinking of Galilee.

## The Sage and the Carpenter

I hear of water seeking the lowest place, and I remember a teacher kneeling with a towel around his waist to wash his disciples' feet.

I hear of the wise person who does not compete, and I remember the one who told his friends that whoever wished to become greatest should become a servant.

I hear of *wu wei*, of acting without forcing, and I remember seeds growing secretly in the earth while the farmer sleeps.

I hear warnings about grasping after wealth and reputation, and I remember lilies that do not toil and birds that do not fill storehouses.

I hear of returning, yielding, becoming like a newborn child—and somewhere nearby I hear an invitation to become like little children and enter a kingdom that cannot be seized by force.

These are not identical teachings.

I do not want to make them identical.

Laozi arose from ancient Chinese soil; Jesus from Jewish soil. The *Dao De Jing* and the Gospels carry different histories, languages, questions, and visions of ultimate reality. To honor either voice requires allowing it to remain itself.

But two melodies do not have to be identical to harmonize.

Sometimes I hear something in Laozi that helps me hear Jesus differently. Sometimes the words of Jesus illuminate a passage of the *Dao De Jing* from an angle I had not considered. And sometimes they simply sit beside one another like two old travelers sharing tea beneath a tree.

That is the spirit in which I wrote these pages.

## An Elder Who Walked Both Roads

The voice speaking through these devotionals is fictional.

I imagined an old Daoist Master who, somewhere along his long journey, encountered Jesus and came to love him. He has wandered bamboo forests and walked the shores of Galilee. He has listened to mountain streams and sat in silent monasteries. He has read Laozi beneath an open sky and heard the parables of Jesus beside the evening fire.

He does not feel compelled to decide which landscape belongs to whom.

Water is water.

Bread is bread.

A seed disappearing into the earth does not ask whether its wisdom is Eastern or Western.

This elder does not argue that Christianity is secretly Daoism or that Daoism is secretly Christianity. He is simply someone who has lived long enough to recognize familiar fragrances in distant gardens.

Perhaps wisdom has made him less interested in fences.

He has discovered that the Sage who refuses to force and the Carpenter who refuses the sword can sometimes illuminate one another. That the empty bowl and the broken bread may sit upon the same table without either ceasing to be itself. That the river flowing toward the sea and the prodigal walking toward home may awaken something similar within the heart.

So he listens.

And then he tells us what he has seen.

## Open Hands

This book is therefore not intended as a systematic comparison of Christianity and Daoism. It is not an argument that the *Dao* is simply another name for the Christian God, nor that every teaching in these traditions can be reconciled.

It is a contemplative experiment.

What happens if a follower of Jesus listens carefully to Laozi without fear?

What happens if we resist the urge either to convert another tradition into our own language or to keep wisdom sealed inside cultural boundaries?

What happens if we simply listen?

I suspect that wisdom is much larger than the containers in which we receive it.

Rain falls upon monastery roofs and bamboo groves. The same moon appears over Jerusalem and ancient China. Human beings everywhere have known grief and desire, ambition and failure, love and death. Everywhere we have looked into the darkness and wondered what holds all of this together.

Perhaps we can learn from one another without surrendering the paths we love.

For me, that means remaining a follower of Jesus while sitting gratefully at the feet of Laozi as a wise teacher.

And perhaps that is enough.

So I invite you to read these pages slowly.

Do not worry about agreeing with everything. Do not hurry to decide whether two traditions are saying precisely the same thing. Let the images remain images for a while.

Sit beside the water.

Watch the bamboo bend.

Listen to the Carpenter tell a story about a seed disappearing into the earth.

Let the old Sage pour another cup of tea.

And perhaps, somewhere between the words, we may hear the quiet movement of a Wisdom that has always been larger than our names for it.

We may even discover that the journey into another tradition has somehow led us more deeply into our own.
